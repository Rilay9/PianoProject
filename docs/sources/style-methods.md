# Blues/boogie-woogie, ragtime and rock/metal keyboard: published method books (source extract)

Scope: for each of the three styles, at most two published piano method books (a structured course, not a song collection), taken from the publishers' own pages, contents lists and sample pages. Citation style follows `docs/sources/methods.md`. This file extracts; it proposes no rungs, placements, pieces or curriculum changes.

**Date accessed (every document below):** 2026-10-09.

**How it was read.** Publisher pages were fetched and their text read directly (HTML to text), so contents lists below are verbatim where marked "verbatim". Hal Leonard "Closer Look" sample pages are page images (cover, contents, a few content pages) and were read from images ("read from images"); the image URLs are given. Wording attributed to a content page of a book is a paraphrase of that page unless in quote marks. "(inferred)" marks anything not stated outright. "Not stated" means the sources read say nothing on the point. "Not publicly available" means no readable contents were found. Page numbers are the printed book pages where the source shows them (Hal Leonard contents pages); Schott's German page lists contents lines without page numbers, so those are cited as "contents line N" (position in the list as printed on the page).

**Not read by anything in this file:** the Schott sample PDF `preview_275111.pdf` and `preview_585609.pdf` on Schott's S3 bucket (HTTP 403 when fetched, with and without a Referer header); IMSLP's scan of Joplin's *School of Ragtime* (IMSLP returned a "verify you are human" bot check, not bypassed); the Schott product page for the English *Discovering Boogie & Blues Piano* (the URL named in the brief, and its /de/ and /en/ variants, return HTTP 404 as fetched).

# Part 1. Blues (with boogie-woogie)

Searches: five queries across Hal Leonard, Schott and general (Alfred and Mel Bay surfaced no blues or boogie method with a readable contents list). Two books extracted (B1, B2).

Found but not extracted (cap of two): Hal Leonard *Boogie-Woogie Piano* (Todd Lowry, HL 117067, 72 pp., https://www.halleonard.com/product/117067/boogie-woogie-piano) shows only its cover and back cover, which list "Harmonic Patterns; Bass Lines; Melodic Riffs; Playing Popular Melodies; Improvisation Ideas; Boogie-Woogie History"; no contents page is public. Hal Leonard *Teach Yourself to Play Blues & Boogie Piano* (Michael Tarro, HL 248990) lists topics only (12-bar blues, left-hand bass patterns, right-hand licks, turnarounds and endings, swing and dotted rhythms, improvisation) and is aimed at "intermediate to early advanced" players; no contents page is public. Hal Leonard *Boogie Woogie For Beginners* (Frank Paparelli, HL 312559) lists four pieces and says it has left-hand bass exercises and 25 bass patterns; no concept sequence is public.

## B1. Wierzyk, *Boogie & Blues Piano* (Schott)

- **Stated level:** "Level of difficulty: intermediate" (B1 page, product-details field).
- **Description (B1 page, German):** a classroom-tested programme that shows, in "systematisch aufgebauten Lektionen", the way to blues and boogie pianist; audio tracks combine fast original versions and slower practice versions. The lessons are numbered "Boogie 1a" to "Boogie 15c".
- **English edition.** The brief's candidate, *Discovering Boogie & Blues Piano* (Schott Music London, ED 13246, with CD), is not readable here (page 404; sample PDF 403). A web-search summary (not a Schott page I read) says it is the English translation of the German book; whether its contents match B1 is not established. Treat B1 as the readable contents of the same author's method.

## B2. Harrison, *Blues Piano* (Hal Leonard Keyboard Style Series)

- **Stated level:** not stated as a grade or "beginner". The back cover says the book "will teach you the basic skills you need to start playing the blues" and ends with "seven complete tunes to jam on" (BC image). The product page repeats the claim (B2 product page).
- **Back-cover list:** Scales & Chords; Left-Hand Patterns; Walking Bass; Comping; Endings & Turnarounds; Right-Hand Techniques; How to Solo with Blues Scales; Crossover Licks.

**What the sample pages show (read from images):**
- p.6: the major scale taught as whole and half steps (W W H W W W H), then all twelve major scales shown, "most closely parallels how the ear relates to the scale".
- p.15: the 12-bar blues in C with chords written I7, IV7, V7 (C7, F7, G7); three four-bar sections; bars 11-12 are the turnaround, "simplest options are to stay on the I7, or to move to the V7 in bar 12".

Groups two to four, pp.18-22: contents titles only; text not public.
- Everything else beyond the contents titles (the specific right-hand licks, the order of the seven tunes' difficulty): not publicly available.

# Part 2. Ragtime

Searches: six queries (Hal Leonard, Alfred, Schott, Mel Bay, FJH; general). **No stand-alone ragtime method book with a readable contents list was found.** What exists is below.

## R1. Valerio, *Stride & Swing Piano* (Hal Leonard Keyboard Style Series): ragtime is Chapter 1 of a style survey

- **Stated level:** not stated. The back cover says it "covers all the essential left- and right-hand techniques of early solo jazz piano playing - chords, bass, runs, patterns, etc. - and includes 14 full songs" (BC image). Hal Leonard's product page: "teaches left- and right-hand techniques including chords, bass runs, patterns and more".
- It is a survey of seven styles, each chapter tied to one player. Contents (read from image, printed pages): 1 Classic Ragtime (Scott Joplin) p.4; 2 Blues & Boogie Woogie (Jimmy Yancey & Pete Johnson) p.18; 3 New Orleans Jazz (Jelly Roll Morton) p.26; 4 Stride Piano I (James P. Johnson) p.35; 5 Stride Piano II (Fats Waller) p.44; 6 Swing Piano I (Teddy Wilson) p.61; 7 Swing Piano II (Art Tatum) p.78. The ragtime chapter is therefore pp.4-17.
- **What the two ragtime sample pages show (read from images):**
  - p.4: ragtime arose from two styles, the cakewalk and the march. A cakewalk's right hand has a syncopated, repetitive melodic rhythm (the page marks syncopations with asterisks); the left hand has a steady bass-note/chord alternation "derived from the march", described as an "oom-pah, oom-pah" motion. "Thus the seeds of ragtime were sown": a syncopated right hand against a steady left-hand pulse. The page says ragtime's rhythmic complexity "is not easily explained by European concepts like syncopation". "Classic ragtime" is distinguished from later "Eastern ragtime".
  - p.12 (the chapter's own sample rag; title omitted here): form AABBCCDD; the C section is in C major and the trio is in the subdominant (F major); the right hand plays "additive rhythm groupings" that often do not coincide with the left-hand pulse; chromatic chords toward the final cadence of each strain; to be played "in a moderate tempo", with evenness in both hands; "Classic ragtime should not swing in the modern sense of the word". Written 2/4 in C, left hand bass note then chord.
  - The order of topics between pp.5-11 and 13-17 (for example the teaching of the left-hand patterns and rag form): not publicly available.
- **Stride (Chapters 4-5)**, in case stride/oom-pah left hand is wanted: only p.44 is readable: later stride keeps "the left hand kept the pulse as before and bass-note/chord alternation was still the norm, with occasional walking bass lines and melodic passages"; sixth and seventh chords become common; chord voicings often four notes; "common major sixth voicings" shown in all twelve keys. Chapter 4 (Early Stride) text: not publicly available.

## R2. Joplin, *School of Ragtime* (six exercises, 1908)

Published instructional manual by Scott Joplin; IMSLP files it under "Methods; Exercises; Studies", 6 exercises, 3 pp. scan, public domain (IMSLP page text read). Schott's *20 Ragtimes for Piano* says the edition "contains a 'School of Ragtime' written by Joplin himself, in which the stylistics and special playing techniques of ragtime are explained and illustrated with practical playing exercises" (Hal Leonard product page text; level for the whole volume: "Intermediate-Advanced"). **The text of the exercises is not readable here** (IMSLP bot check; no Schott or Hal Leonard sample pages found for it). Contents: not publicly available through the sources used.

## R3. Frank, *Rhythm-Styles for Piano* (Schott Pro Line): ragtime as one pattern chapter

Part 1 of the German contents list (verbatim) is: Einleitung; Uebe-Tipps; Rhythm Patterns: Walzer; Ragtime; Country & Western; Foxtrott; Charleston; Tango; Rumba; Beguine; Swing; Shuffle; Cha Cha Cha; Mambo; La Bamba; Samba; Bossa Nova; Lambada; Literaturtipps. The page says each style is "explained with an introduction to the style's history and evolution, analysis and exercises" (description, English text on the page). It is a style-patterns book across 27 styles (with Boogie Woogie, Rhythm & Blues, Rock'n'Roll and others in Part 2); it is not a ragtime course, and what its Ragtime chapter teaches or in what order: not publicly available (no sample pages read).

# Part 3. Rock and metal keyboard

Searches: five queries across Hal Leonard, Schott and others. Two books extracted (K1, K2). K2 is the only book found that has a heavy-metal chapter; neither book is a metal-only method (none found; Hal Leonard's heavy-metal line is guitar, bass and drums in the results).

Found but not extracted (cap of two): Schott *Discovering Rock Piano* Vol. 1 (Juergen Moser, "Chords, Rhythms, Melodies and Improvisation", Schott Pop-Styles Vol. 1, ED 13069D Q585609, 132 pp., level "easy", English, https://www.schott-music.com/en/discovering-rock-piano-noq585609.html) and Vol. 2 (ED 13070D Q585610, 135 pp., level "intermediate", https://www.schott-music.com/en/discovering-rock-piano-noq585610.html): both pages carry full printed contents lists (Vol. 1: Playing Chords, Rhythm, Melody and Improvisation; Vol. 2: From Boogie to Rock, Solo Studies, Creative Keyboard-Playing); neither mentions metal; they were read but not extracted here because of the two-book cap.

- **Stated level:** Hal Leonard: it "will teach you the basic skills needed to play beginning rock keyboard. From comping to soloing, you'll learn the theory, tools, and techniques used by the pros" (product page).

**What the sample pages show (read from images):**
- p.4 ("What Is Rock Music?"): rock history and sub-styles (blues-rock, classic rock, hard rock) with notable keyboardists; it also says hard rock spawned "a whole series of heavy metal bands" from the 1980s (text only, no playing content).
- p.13 ("Power Chord Voicings and Comping"): borrows the guitarists' "power chord" idea: only the root and 5th of the chord (chord symbols with a "5" suffix, e.g. C5, G5, F5); the right hand's top note is the root or 5th, chosen for smooth voice leading, and doubled an octave lower with the thumb; the example is I-V-IV-I in C, eighth-note driving right hand accenting beat 1, the "& of 2" and beat 4, left hand following the roots; "commonly used in harder rock and metal styles". The preceding text (p.13, top) describes an I-vi-IV-V (C-Am-F-G) pop/rock progression with right-hand triad inversions accenting the upbeats and a left-hand eighth-note octave pattern.
- Pages 6-12 and 14-58 other than the contents page: not publicly available (the rest of Chapter 2 and the later chapters were not read).

## K2. Miller, *Rock Keyboard: The Complete Guide* (Hal Leonard Keyboard Style Series)

- **Stated level:** the introduction says "Whatever your playing level - beginning, intermediate, or advanced - you'll find something here that meets your needs" (p.2). Hal Leonard's product page: "Learn to comp or solo in any of your favorite rock styles"; styles: classic rock, pop/rock, blues rock, Southern rock, hard rock, progressive rock, alternative rock and heavy metal.
- **Design of every chapter (p.2, introduction):** each chapter covers one style; its first section is *comping* (harmony, chord voicings, bass lines), its second *soloing* (licks, riffs, fills, improvising with scales and chords). Most parts are two-handed but may be played one-handed; the bass parts typically double a bassist; the book recommends transposing the examples to other keys.

**Sequence, from the printed contents page (read from image):** 1 Classic Rock p.4; 2 Pop Rock p.13; 3 Blues Rock p.19; 4 Southern Rock p.27; 5 Hard Rock p.33; 6 Progressive Rock p.39; 7 Alternative Rock p.45; 8 Heavy Metal p.49; Scales p.56. Within-chapter headings other than those on the sample pages: not publicly available.

**What the sample pages show (read from images):**
- p.4 (Chapter 1, Classic Rock), Comping: "Four-Bar Vamp (with Turnaround)": a vamp on I and V dominant sevenths (G7, D7) with three-note right-hand inversions, bass on the chord roots with chromatic approaches, an "anticipated" feel (the "and" of beat 2 anticipates beat 3); then "Two-Bar Vamps" repeated to form an eight-bar phrase with a fill that mixes blues and pentatonic ideas (flat 3rd, flat 5th, 6th).
- p.13 (Chapter 2, Pop Rock), Comping: "Octave Bass" (right-hand triads over a left-hand eighth-note octave bass; I-bVII-IV in G) and "Pedal Point" (bass stays on G while chords change above).
- p.49 (Chapter 8, Heavy Metal), Comping: "Right-Hand Clusters (Texture)": a minor/Dorian feel over an implied D pedal; the keyboard holds long whole-note chord clusters (suspensions: second and fourth intervals over the Dm7/D area) as a pad, with an optional eighth-note bass line that "jams modally" and takes a flat 5th from the D blues scale. The chapter introduction names the keyboardist's tools in metal as "dissonant pads, unusual progressions, sixteenth-note punches, minor riffs". Metal soloing section and "Scales" (p.56): not publicly available.
