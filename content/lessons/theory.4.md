**The circle of fifths** arranges the twelve keys so each is a fifth above the
last: C G D A E B F♯/G♭ D♭ A♭ E♭ B♭ F, back to C. Sharps increase clockwise,
flats anticlockwise, and neighbours on the circle share all but one note — which
is exactly why music modulates to the key next door and almost never to the key
opposite.

Read it as a map of relatedness, not a list to memorise. The relative minor of
each key sits inside the circle, a minor third below.

**Inversions by ear.** Root position sounds solid; first inversion (third in the
bass) sounds lighter and leans forward; second inversion (fifth in the bass)
sounds unstable and usually wants to resolve. Play the three positions of one
chord repeatedly and listen only to the bottom note — that is what your ear is
actually tracking.

**Cadences** are the punctuation of harmony, and there are four to know:

- **Authentic (perfect)**: V–I. A full stop.
- **Half**: anything–V. A comma; the phrase is unfinished.
- **Plagal**: IV–I. The "amen" — a soft ending.
- **Deceptive (interrupted)**: V–vi. You expect home and get the relative minor
  instead; the phrase carries on.

Identifying these by ear is the single most useful listening skill on this
track, because it tells you where you are in a piece without counting bars.

**Melodic dictation.** The app plays eight notes; you play them back. Their
names stay off the card until you have answered, so there is nothing to read.
Method: get the *first note* and the *shape* (up/down/same) before
worrying about exact pitches. Then fill in the intervals.

**Common mistake.** Trying to transcribe note by note from the start. Contour
first, then detail — the same order in which you would sketch a face.

**Tools for this rung.** Melodic dictation asks you to hold eight notes before
you can play any of them, and the chain game is that memory on its own, one
note longer each round. *Simon* is the button for it, and from this rung on it opens the version drawn
from every key — *Simon — every key around middle C*, which this rung lists as
an exercise of its own: no scale to fall back on, and the note has to come back in the octave
it was played in. That one plays by ear and lights nothing — until you miss,
when it plays the chain again over the lit keys and asks you for the same chain
rather than ending the game.

**How you'll know you've got it.** Cadences identified at 80 % by ear, triad
inversions played from their slash-chord names, and an eight-note phrase played
back correctly after two hearings.
