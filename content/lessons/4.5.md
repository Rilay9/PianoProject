Every time signature so far has divided the beat in two. Now it divides in
three, and beats stop landing where you expect.

**6/8 is compound time.** It looks like six eighth-note beats and it is not: it
is **two beats, each a dotted quarter, each divided into three eighths**. Count
"1 2 3 4 5 6" while learning, then switch to "ONE-two-three FOUR-five-six" with
weight on 1 and 4, and finally to just "1 2" with a triple lilt underneath. If
you keep counting all six at tempo the music will plod. *Row Row Row Your Boat*
and *Greensleeves in 6/8* are both classic examples — long-short, long-short.

**A triplet** is three notes played in the time of two, marked with a small 3.
It is the same three-against-two feel, borrowed into simple time for a bar or
two. Say "trip-a-let" evenly.

**Swing** is a performance convention, not a notation: written straight eighth
notes are played long-short, roughly in a 2:1 ratio, as if the pair were the
first and third notes of a triplet. Nothing on the page says so except the word
"swing" or "shuffle" at the top. This is where the blues track begins.

**Syncopation** puts the accent where the beat is not — on an "and", or on a
note tied over the beat so nothing is struck on the downbeat at all. Reading it
is easier if you keep counting the beats out loud and let the notes fall between
them, rather than trying to feel the rhythm as a shape.

**What to do at the piano.** Rhythm reading in 6/8 and with syncopation,
sight-reading level 3, then *Row Row Row Your Boat* and *Greensleeves in 6/8*
for the compound feel.

**Common mistake.** Playing 6/8 as though it were 3/4 with fast notes — six
equal weights instead of two groups of three. Tap the two main beats with your
foot while you play.

**How you'll know you've got it.** One 6/8 piece and one syncopated piece, both
to tempo, with your foot on beats one and four the whole way through.
