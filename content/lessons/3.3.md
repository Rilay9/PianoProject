**A minor triad** is a major triad with its middle note lowered a half step. C–E–G
is C major; C–E flat–G is C minor. That one semitone is the whole difference
between the two moods, and it is worth playing the pair a few times just to hear
how small the change is.

**The relative minor.** A minor uses exactly the same notes as C major — no
sharps, no flats — but treats A as home instead of C. Every major key has a
relative minor a third below it: C/Am, G/Em, F/Dm. They share a key signature, so
when you see "no sharps" you have to decide from the music which of the two it
is. Look at the first and last bass notes; they usually tell you.

**Natural versus harmonic minor.** Play A to A on the white keys and it sounds
modal, old, unresolved — that is **natural minor**. The problem is the G: a whole
step below A, it does not pull home. **Harmonic minor** raises it to G sharp, a
half step below the tonic, and suddenly the scale has a leading tone and the
key has gravity. That raised seventh is not in the key signature; it appears as
an accidental every time it is used.

**Where it shows up.** The V chord of A minor is E, and with a G natural it
would be E minor, which resolves weakly. Raise the seventh and you get **E
major**, or better **E7** (E–G♯–B–D). *Greensleeves* is the classic
demonstration: the tune is in A minor, the accompaniment is Am and G, and every
cadence turns to E7 with its G sharp.

**What to do at the piano.** A harmonic minor scale hands separately, Am–Dm–E7
changes, and the major-versus-minor ear drill. Then *Greensleeves (with chords)*.

**Common mistake.** Playing the raised seventh going up and forgetting it coming
down, or the reverse. In harmonic minor it is raised in both directions;
melodic minor is the one that changes, and that is Stage 4.

**How you'll know you've got it.** One minor-key song hands together, and you
can hear a major and a minor triad as different before you look at them.
