The keyboard looks like eighty-eight identical problems. It is actually one
pattern of twelve keys repeated seven and a bit times, and once you see the
pattern you never have to count from the end of the keyboard again.

**The black keys come in groups of two and three.** That is the whole map. Find
any group of two black keys: the white key immediately to its *left* is **C**.
Find any group of three: the white key immediately to its left is **F**. Those
two landmarks are enough to name every other key, because the white keys run
C D E F G A B and then start again at C.

**An octave** is the distance from one C to the next C — twelve keys, counting
black and white. Two notes an octave apart sound like the same note high and
low, which is why they share a letter name.

**Middle C** is the C nearest the middle of the instrument, usually the one just
left of the maker's name. In MIDI, the language your HP-130 speaks to PianoPath,
middle C is note number 60. Everything the app says about pitch is measured from
there, and you will see C4 used as its name: the fourth C from the bottom.

**Higher is right, lower is left.** Obvious at the piano, less obvious on the
page — but it is the same direction, and reading music is much easier once you
stop translating and just move the way the notes move.

**What to do at the piano.** Say the letter out loud as you play it. Play every
C from bottom to top, then every F, then every G. Then let the app name a key
and find it; you are aiming for speed of *recognition*, not speed of hand.

**Common mistake.** Counting up from C every time you need a G. That works and
it is slow forever. Learn G as "the middle key of the three-black-key group's
left neighbourhood" — that is, look at the shape, not the count.

**How you'll know you've got it.** Twenty named keys in forty seconds, anywhere
on the keyboard, at 95 % accuracy, without looking at your hands to count.
