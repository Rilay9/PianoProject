The right pedal lifts the dampers off every string at once, so notes keep
sounding after you let the keys go. It is not a volume pedal and it is not a
legato substitute — it is a way of connecting harmony that the hand cannot
reach, and it blurs anything you leave under it.

**Legato pedalling** — sometimes called syncopated pedalling — is the technique
that matters. The rule is counter-intuitive: **change the pedal just *after* the
new chord sounds, not with it.** Play the chord, and a fraction of a second
later lift the pedal and put it straight back down. The old harmony is released,
the new one is caught, and there is no gap. If you change *with* the chord you
get a hole; if you change late you get a smear.

Foot technique: heel stays on the floor, the ball of the foot stays in contact
with the pedal at all times. Lifting the foot off makes noise and loses control.

**Pedal marks** are either the old "Ped." with a star for release, or a
horizontal bracket whose notch marks each change. Many editions of the pieces
here have none at all, and then the rule is: change with the harmony.

**What your HP-130 sends.** Sustain is MIDI controller 64 (CC64). PianoPath
reads it, shows a pedal lamp, and scores the *timing* of your changes — it can
tell you when two chords overlapped by more than 120 ms, which is exactly the
smear you cannot hear in yourself while playing.

**What to do at the piano.** The pedal-change drill on I–IV–V (listen for a
clean change, not a clean foot), broken-chord left hand with pedal, then
*Greensleeves (waltz bass)* and the easy *Canon in D*.

**Common mistake.** Holding the pedal down through a chord change because it
sounds fuller. Play it once with the pedal held through and once with clean
changes, and listen to the difference — after that you will hear it every time.

**How you'll know you've got it.** The pedal drill at 90 % clean changes, with
no overlap longer than 120 ms.
