This track is the other way of playing the piano. Instead of reading every note,
you read a chord symbol and decide the notes yourself — which is how almost all
popular, folk and church music is actually played, and how you play with other
people.

**A lead sheet** has one line of melody with chord symbols above it. That is the
whole score. Everything else — voicing, rhythm, which hand plays what — is your
arrangement, and two players will produce two different pieces from the same
page.

**The starting arrangement**: melody in the right hand, chord in the left, one
chord per bar, played on beat one and held. It is dull and it works, and every
richer texture is a variation on it.

**Left-hand rhythms** are the first variation. Instead of one chord on beat one,
try: chord on 1 and 3; root on 1 and chord on 2, 3, 4; or root–chord–chord in
3/4. Match the rhythm to the song, not to your habit — a hymn wants held chords,
a folk song wants a pulse.

**I, IV and V in five keys.** C, G, F, D and A. Learn the three chords of each
as a *shape you can find without thinking*, because the moment you have to work
out what the IV of D is, you have stopped playing. D: D, G, A7. A: A, D, E7.

**Transposing.** Because you are reading roles rather than notes, moving a song
to another key is a matter of renaming three chords. This is worth practising
explicitly: play *When the Saints* in C, then F, then G, from the same page.

**Repertoire for this rung.** *When the Saints* in C and F, *Happy Birthday*,
*Jingle Bells* with block chords in C and in G, *Greensleeves* with chords.

**Common mistake.** Playing every chord in root position and leaping around.
Stage 3.2's voice leading applies here immediately — use inversions to keep the
left hand still.

**How you'll know you've got it.** You can play a song you know from a chord
chart, in two different keys, without written-out notation.
