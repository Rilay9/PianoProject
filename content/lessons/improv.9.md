One piece, finished. Two or three minutes. Written down well enough that
somebody else could play it.

**Form is the decision you make first.** ABA is enough. A is the idea, B is
something that contrasts with it — quieter, or in another key, or with a
different left hand — and the return of A is the whole reason B was worth having.

**Write the ending early.** Pieces stall at the end because the end was never
decided. Write the last four bars in the first week and compose towards them.

**Take it down from yourself.** Improvise until something is worth keeping, then
work out what you played, phrase by phrase, the same way you would take down
somebody else's tune. That is what the ear drill has been training.

**What to practise.** Twenty minutes a day on the same piece, for as long as it
takes. Not a new idea every day.

**Repertoire for this rung.** Your piece.

**Tools for this rung.** One button, *Free play*: no piece, no marking, no
record kept. It names a chord from three or more notes held down together, so
when you are taking a phrase down from yourself and the harmony under it will
not come, hold the notes and let the screen name what you played. Nothing else
here has a button, and that is the right shape for a rung whose repertoire is a
piece that does not exist yet.

**Common mistake.** Starting again. The second piece is not better than the first
one finished.

**How you'll know you've got it.** It is written down, it is playable, and you
are prepared to let someone hear it.
