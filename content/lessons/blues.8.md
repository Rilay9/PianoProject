A blues you can play in C is a blues you can play. A blues you can play in E
flat at two in the morning is a blues you know.

**Transposing the form.** The twelve bars are I, IV and V, so transposing them is
not a memory job — it is knowing where I, IV and V are in the new key, which the
roman-numeral work on the theory track has already given you. Do it slowly in
one new key rather than badly in six.

**Ninths.** The blues is built on dominant sevenths, and every one of them takes
a ninth without asking. C9 rather than C7 for the whole form is the single
cheapest way to make a blues sound less like an exercise. The thirteenth works
too and is what you hear on records.

**Comping over your own bass.** By this stage the left hand can walk without
supervision, which frees the right hand to play a rhythm rather than chords on
beats. Four to the floor is fine and is not lazy; the off-beat pattern is harder
and is where the feel lives.

**What to practise.** The form in two new keys. Then the same form with ninths
throughout, in the key you started in.

**Repertoire for this rung.** Your own chorus, written down. Twelve bars, in two
keys.

**Common mistake.** Transposing by moving your hands the same distance. That is
how the flat keys stay unlearned; read the numerals and find the notes.

**How you'll know you've got it.** Twelve bars in a flat key, with ninths, from
memory.
