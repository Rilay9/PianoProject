title: "Trading fours, and a set list you both know"

Two people who can both comp and both walk a bass still need two more things
before an evening works: a way of handing the solo back and forth, and an agreed
list of tunes.

**Trading fours is a conversation with a rule.** Four bars are yours, the next
four are theirs, and the form keeps going underneath whether either of you has
anything to say. You cannot stop to think, you cannot run over, and you have to
come in on the first beat of your own four — which is harder than it sounds,
because you spent the previous four listening rather than counting.

**Start with two, not four.** Two bars is short enough that you have to have
decided before it arrives. Play one idea. A held note and a rest beats a
scramble, and it leaves the other player something to reply to.

**What the chart already tells you.** These are band parts, and the band's
instructions are still printed on them: which letter the solos start at, where
somebody takes a two-bar break, and at the end a tag or a turnaround to send it
round again. Read those before you play a note — they are the plan two people
would otherwise have to agree out loud.

**The keys are not the guitarist's.** Four of these five sit in flat keys,
because that is where the horns played them in 1920, and the guitarist's keys
are E, A, D and G. One of the five is written with a sharp in the signature and
changes key partway through. There are two honest answers, and this rung wants
you to try both: move the chart to a guitar key by numerals, or leave it and let
the guitarist be the one who works.

**Repertoire for this rung.** Five options, every one a lead sheet on a single
stave with its chords printed. *After You've Gone* is the shortest at thirty-six
bars, and the one you have already met on the jazz ladder. *Jazz Me Blues* prints three
one-bar breaks as well as the solo letter. *Weary Blues* is the one that changes
key, and carries a direction sending you back to the top. *Riverside Blues*
names its two breaks — one in unison, one for the clarinet — and tells you what
to play behind the lead. *Storyville Blues* is the biggest — fifty-six bars, four flats
and seventy-five chord changes — and it prints a tag ending. Each row here has a
*Chart* beside it, which opens the changes with the bar count kept and no notes
in the way.

**Tools for this rung.** The lab's *Blues — twelve bars* preset gives you the
bed to practise this against on your own. Press *Jam it*, then set *Trading
fours* to 2 bars each: the app takes the first two bars and hands you the next
two, round and round. Afterwards it says whether you came in inside your own
bars and how many of your notes were in the blues scale. Nothing is recorded and
nothing here can be passed or failed — it is a mirror, not a mark.

**Common mistake.** Playing through your four bars *and* theirs. Trading works
because of the silence on your side; a pianist who keeps comping underneath the
other player's four has turned a conversation back into an accompaniment.

**How you'll know you've got it.** Eight trades in a row at a steady tempo, in
on the beat every time, with at least one of them being a single idea rather
than a run.
