**Not bundled.** In copyright; import your own MusicXML.

**What the piano part has to do.** A mid-tempo song in a major key with a
country-rock lilt, where the keyboard's job is accompaniment rather than melody:
broken chords and open voicings under a vocal, with the harmonic rhythm slow
enough that you have time to think.

**The techniques.**

- **Broken-chord accompaniment in a major key.** Root, fifth, third, fifth in the
  left hand, or the whole chord spread across both hands. Stage 3.6's patterns,
  applied.
- **Open voicings.** Rather than three notes in a fist, spread the chord: root
  low in the left hand, third and fifth up an octave in the right. It sounds
  bigger and leaves room for a voice.
- **Playing and singing.** The reason to simplify. If you cannot sing over what
  you are playing, the part is too busy for the song.

**Public-domain vehicles.** *Greensleeves (waltz bass)* and the chords-and-pop
Stage 3 material for the accompaniment habit; *Row Row Row Your Boat* in 6/8 if
the song's feel is compound rather than straight — check the recording.

**What to listen for in the recording.** Whether the keyboard is playing on the
beat or between the beats, and whether it doubles the guitar's chord voicing or
sits below it. Count how often the chord changes: if it is once a bar, the part
is simpler than it sounds.

**Common mistake.** Adding fills. In a song with a vocal, the piano's job is
almost entirely to stay out of the way and keep the harmony moving.

**How you'll know you've got it.** You can play the accompaniment pattern for
three minutes without a mistake while humming the melody, at the song's tempo.
