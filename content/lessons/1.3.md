The left hand gets its own five keys and its own clef.

**Left-hand C position** is C3 D3 E3 F3 G3 — the octave below middle C — with
**finger 5 on C3 and finger 1 (thumb) on G3**. The fingering runs the opposite
way round from the right hand because the hands are mirror images: in both
hands, 5 is the outside finger and 1 points inwards.

**The bass clef** is the curled symbol with two dots. The dots sit either side
of the second line down, and that line is **F** — "Bass F", the F below middle
C. Learn that one landmark and count from it. Two more landmarks make reading
fast: **C3** is the second space up from the bottom, and **middle C** sits on a
ledger line just *above* the bass staff, mirroring its position just below the
treble staff. Middle C is the hinge between the two clefs.

**Why the same letters look different.** A note on the middle line is B in the
treble clef and D in the bass clef. That is not a trick played on you; a clef's
whole job is to say which pitches the lines mean, so the same five lines can
cover a piano's range without a hundred ledger lines.

**What to do at the piano.** Walk the left hand up and down 5-4-3-2-1-2-3-4-5,
saying the letters. Then bass-clef note flash from F2 to C4. Then the left-hand
versions of *Hot Cross Buns*, *Mary Had a Little Lamb*, *Ode to Joy* and
*Old MacDonald* — the same tunes you already know, so your ear can tell you when
your reading is wrong.

**Common mistake.** Reading bass-clef notes by working out the treble name and
shifting. It works for a month and then becomes the ceiling on your sight
reading. Learn Bass F as its own fact.

**How you'll know you've got it.** One left-hand tune at 60 bpm at 90 %, and you
can name any bass-clef note between F2 and C4 within two seconds.
