Stage 5 taught the two ideas ragtime is made of: a metronomic left hand and a
right hand that lands between its beats. This rung is where you play a whole
rag as it was printed — four or five strains, a key change in the middle, and a
left hand that has to travel further than it did before.

**A rag is a set of tunes, not one tune.** Joplin wrote in sixteen-bar strains,
usually laid out `Intro · AA · BB · A · CC · DD`. Each strain is a complete idea
with its own character; there is no development, no working-out, nothing that
depends on what came before. That is good news for practice: **learn a rag one
strain at a time and you are learning four short pieces, not one long one.** Get
strain A to tempo before you look at B. A rag you can play three strains of is
worth far more than a rag you can stumble through all of.

**The trio and its key change.** The trio — the strain after the middle — almost
always moves to the subdominant, a fourth up, which on the flat side means one
more flat in the key signature. All three rags here do it: *The Entertainer*
goes from C to F, *Peacherine Rag* from E flat to A flat, *The Easy Winners*
from A flat to D flat. Mark the change on the page. Then find the note that is
newly flattened — D flat in *Peacherine*'s trio, G flat in *The Easy Winners*' —
because playing it natural, with the hand still in the previous key, is the
commonest wrong note in a whole rag.

**Flat keys are the real new skill here.** *The Entertainer* stays on the easy
side of the key signature — C, with one flat in the trio. The other two live in
three to five flats, and reading fluently in E flat and A flat is a different
job from transposing into them. The scale
exercises on this rung exist for that reason: play the A flat and E flat scales
hands together, two octaves, until the black keys stop being a surprise, and
your reading will follow. Notice that the *fingering* is often easier in flat
keys than in C — the long fingers sit naturally on the black keys — so this is
mostly a reading problem, not a hand problem.

**The left hand travels further now.** Stage 5's oom-pah was a bass note and a
chord about an octave apart. In a full rag you will meet:

- **tenths** instead of octaves in the bass note, sometimes rolled;
- **walk-ups** — a scale or chromatic run in the bass between chords;
- **octave leaps of a tenth or more** in the second and fourth strains, where the
  bass note drops and the chord has to land in the same half-beat.

The technique is the same one Stage 5 named and it does not change: **the arm
travels early, and the eye goes to the chord before the hand does.** Practise
the left hand alone at a tempo where you never miss, then take it up by
five clicks at a time. If you are missing leaps at speed, you took it up too
fast; there is no other cause.

**"Not fast" is printed on the music.** These editions carry Joplin's own
markings — *Not fast* on *The Entertainer*, *Not too fast* on *Peacherine Rag*,
*Not fast* on *The Easy Winners* — and the app takes its default tempo from
them. Ragtime played fast stops sounding syncopated, because syncopation is
only audible against a pulse you can hear. Play them slower than you think you
should. **And never swing them:** the eighths and sixteenths are dead even.

**Reading the printed page.** Rags use repeat marks and *D.C.* far more than
anything you have played so far, and the road map matters — a rag played in the
wrong order is not the piece. Before you play a note, trace the route through
the page with a finger and say the strain letters out loud.

**Repertoire for this rung.** Three complete rags, any one of which counts:

- ***The Entertainer*** (1902) — the one you already half know from Stage 5, now
  complete: intro, A, A, B, B, A, then the trio in F and a last strain back in
  C. The easiest way in, because the form is the only thing that is new.
- ***Peacherine Rag*** (1901) — E flat, with a strain in B flat and a trio in
  A flat. Gentler syncopation than *The Entertainer* but a harder key, so it
  trades one problem for another.
- ***The Easy Winners*** (1901) — A flat, four strains, a rag two-step. The most
  work of the three and the most rewarding; its name is a joke.

**Common mistake.** Playing hands together from bar one. At this length that
means learning four strains badly at once. Left hand alone, one strain, until it
is automatic — every time.

**How you'll know you've got it.** One complete rag, all its strains, hands
together, at the tempo printed on the music, with the left hand steady enough
that a metronome would have nothing to say.
