Charles-Louis Hanon's exercises are sixty patterns, each an eight-note cell that
climbs the keyboard step by step for two octaves and comes back down. They are
useful and they are widely misused, so it is worth being precise about what they
do.

**What they do.** They give you a long, unvarying stream of notes in which the
only variable is *you*. That makes unevenness audible — the weak fourth finger,
the thumb that thumps, the little finger that arrives late — and audible is the
first step to fixable. They also build a physical vocabulary of common finger
sequences that turn up inside real pieces.

**What they do not do.** They do not build strength; your fingers are already
strong enough for a piano. They do not make you musical, and no amount of them
substitutes for repertoire. And they will injure you if you play them with a
locked wrist for an hour.

**Rules that make them safe and useful.**

1. **Never play through pain**, ever, in anything. Tingling or aching means stop
   for the day.
2. **Keep the wrist loose.** Let it move slightly with the contour of the
   pattern. A rigid wrist over hundreds of repeated notes is how people get hurt.
3. **Slow and even beats fast and ragged.** Set the metronome at 40 and raise it
   only when every note is the same length and volume.
4. **Transpose them.** Hanon wrote them in C. Playing number 1 in D flat is worth
   more than playing it in C ten more times, because the black keys change the
   hand shape.

**What to do at the piano.** Hanon 1 to 10 from the generator, any key, from 40
to 108 bpm, hands together. Five minutes at the start of a session is plenty.

**Common mistake.** Treating Hanon as the practice rather than the warm-up. If
technique is eating your session, the balance is wrong.

**How you'll know you've got it.** Numbers 1 to 5 hands together at 80 bpm at
97 %, with the fourth-finger notes as loud as the others.
