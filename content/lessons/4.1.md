**The circle of fifths** is the order the sharps arrive in. Start at C (no
sharps), go up a fifth to G (one sharp, F♯), up another fifth to D (two: F♯ C♯),
again to A (three: F♯ C♯ G♯), then E, B, F♯. Each new key keeps the previous
sharps and adds one, and the new sharp is always the seventh degree of the new
key. Going the other way — down a fifth to F, B flat, E flat — adds flats in the
order B, E, A, D, G.

This is not trivia. It tells you what the key signature will be before you look,
and it is the map that harmony moves around on for the rest of your playing life.

**Standard fingering.** For C, G, D and A the pattern is the same:
right hand **1-2-3-1-2-3-4-5** going up; left hand **5-4-3-2-1-3-2-1**. The
thumb passes after the third finger in the right hand and the third finger
crosses after the thumb in the left, and because the two hands are mirror images
those two events happen at *different* points in the scale. That mismatch is the
entire difficulty of playing scales hands together.

**Contrary motion is the trick.** Start both thumbs on the same C and move
outwards: now the hands are exact mirrors, the thumb passes happen at the same
moment, and the coordination problem vanishes. Learn each scale in contrary
motion first, then in similar motion — the second is much easier once the first
is fluent.

**What to do at the piano.** Each key: contrary motion one octave, then similar
motion one octave, then two, from 60 up to 100 bpm in eighth notes. Then a
Grade 1 piece — the easy *Für Elise*, or *Ode to Joy (easy variation)*.

**Common mistake.** Practising scales fast and unevenly because they are boring.
An uneven scale is worse than no scale; it trains the unevenness into every
piece. Use the metronome and listen to the thumb notes.

**How you'll know you've got it.** Four scales hands together, two octaves, at
80 bpm in eighth notes, at 95 % — with no bump at the thumb.
