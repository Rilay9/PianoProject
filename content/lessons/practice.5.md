Sooner or later something stops improving. Three runs at the same score, a
passage that has been "nearly there" for a fortnight, a general sense that the
last month achieved nothing.

**This is normal and it is not a sign you have reached your limit.** A plateau
is almost always one of three things, and the useful response is to work out
which.

## One: you are practising the wrong thing

You play the piece from the top, it breaks in the same bar, you go back to the
top. The fix is the previous lesson in this module: find the chunk, loop the
chunk, and stop playing the parts that already work.

## Two: you have automated it wrong

Something learned at speed, or learned with a fingering that does not survive,
is now a habit and habits do not improve with repetition — they entrench. The
fix is to go slower than feels reasonable, decide the fingering deliberately,
and rebuild it. It takes a week and it is the only thing that works.

## Three: it is consolidating

Sometimes nothing is wrong. Learning is not linear, and a fortnight of no
visible progress is often followed by a step up that arrives without warning.
This is the case where the right response is to keep going and change nothing.

## Which one is it

If the mistakes move around, it is one. If the same mistake happens in the same
place every time, it is two. If there are no mistakes and it just is not getting
faster, it is usually three.

## Three things to change

When in doubt, change one variable: **the tempo** (much slower, or briefly much
faster), **the key** (transpose it — it forces you to think rather than recall),
or **the order** (start from the middle, or play it backwards a phrase at a
time).

**Common mistake.** Doing the same practice more intensely. A plateau is
information about the method, not about the effort.

**How you'll know you've got it.** You recognise a plateau within a few days
rather than a few weeks, and you have a change to make rather than a reason to
give up.
