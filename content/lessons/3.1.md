**A half step** is the distance to the very next key, black or white — C to C
sharp, or E to F. **A whole step** is two half steps. Every scale is a recipe of
these two.

**A sharp (♯) raises a note by a half step; a flat (♭) lowers it by one.** The
black keys have two names each: the key between C and D is both C sharp and D
flat, and which name you use depends on the key you are in, not on the sound.
Some white keys take accidentals too — E sharp is F, and it does occur.

**The major scale formula is W–W–H–W–W–W–H.** Start on C and follow it: C (W) D
(W) E (H) F (W) G (W) A (W) B (H) C — all white keys, which is why C major is
the one with nothing in the key signature. Now start on G and follow the same
recipe: G A B C D E, then a whole step from E is F sharp, then a half step to G.
That single F sharp is what makes G major. Start on F: F G A, then a half step
to B flat, then C D E F. One flat.

**A key signature** is those accidentals collected at the front of every line so
they do not have to be written again — one sharp means every F in the piece is
F sharp. An **accidental** written in the music itself applies for the rest of
that bar only, then the key signature takes over again.

**What to do at the piano.** G major and F major scales, hands separately, one
octave. Note-flash with sharps and flats. Then *Ode to Joy in G* and *Twinkle in F*.
The two are worth comparing: Twinkle lands on its B flat over and over, while
Ode to Joy never plays an F sharp at all. The key signature is not a list of
the notes you will meet — it is what tells you which key you are in, and you
hold it in your head whether or not the tune gets round to using it.

**Common mistake.** Reading the key signature at the start and forgetting it by
bar four. Before you play, find every F in the piece and say "sharp" out loud.

**How you'll know you've got it.** G and F major scales hands separately, one
octave, evenly, and you can build a major scale from any starting note by
counting W–W–H–W–W–W–H on the keys.
