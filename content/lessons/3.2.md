The same three jobs — I, IV and V — exist in every key. In **G major** they are
G, C and D; in **F major**, F, B flat and C. Learn them as roles, not as
letters, and a song you know in C becomes a song you can play in any key.

**The dominant seventh.** Take the V chord and add the note a seventh above its
root: G–B–D becomes G–B–D–F. That F is a half step below the tonic's third and
a whole step above its root, and both of those pull inwards, which is why a V7
sounds unfinished until the I arrives. Written as G7, D7, C7. In practice the
left hand often drops the fifth and plays **root, third, seventh** — three notes,
all the tension, and a comfortable stretch.

**Voice leading.** Root-position chords make you jump. Keeping **common tones**
removes the jumps: C (C–E–G) to F is easier as F/C (C–F–A), because C stays put
and the other two fingers move one step. C to G7 is easier as B–F–G. Same
chords, same harmony, a fifth less travel. This is the single biggest
improvement available to a beginner's left hand.

**Chord charts.** A chart gives you the symbol and the beat, and leaves the
voicing to you. Reading one is a different skill from reading notation and it is
the skill that lets you play with other people.

**What to do at the piano.** I–IV–V7 drills in C, G and F, both root position
and the smooth version. Chord-symbol flash. Then *Jingle Bells in G* (G, C and
D7 in root position) and *When the Saints in F* (F, B flat and C7, with a
three-beat upbeat to count in).

**Common mistake.** Learning the smooth voicings without ever learning the root
positions, and then being unable to name what you are playing. Learn both; play
the smooth one.

**How you'll know you've got it.** A chord-chart song hands together at 80 bpm,
changing chords without looking at your left hand.
