**The pentatonic scale** is five notes: in C major, **C D E G A** — the major
scale with the fourth and seventh removed. Those two are the notes that create
friction (the fourth clashes with the tonic chord's third, the seventh is a
leading tone that demands resolution), so taking them out leaves a scale where
nothing can sound wrong over a diatonic progression. It is the reason the black
keys alone (an F sharp pentatonic) sound good over almost anything.

**Over I–vi–IV–V.** The app loops C–Am–F–G. Use C major pentatonic throughout —
all five notes fit all four chords. Now the ear is free and the only questions
left are rhythm, shape and where you stop.

**Answering the phrase.** The app plays a two-bar call; you play a two-bar
answer. Rules that make an answer sound like an answer rather than a new
sentence:

- **Match the rhythm** of the call, at least at the start.
- **End somewhere more settled** than the call did — on C or E if the call ended
  on D or A.
- **Keep the register** the same. Jumping an octave sounds like changing the
  subject.

**Motif development.** Take a three-note idea and do things to it deliberately:
repeat it, move it up a step (**sequence**), turn it upside down (**inversion**),
stretch its rhythm (**augmentation**), compress it. Each of those is a decision
you can make in real time, and having four decisions available is what stops
improvisation drifting.

**Target notes.** Choose the note you are going to land on at the start of the
next bar before you get there. Aiming for a landing point is what turns a run of
notes into a line.

**Common mistake.** Playing the pentatonic scale up and down. A scale played in
order is a scale, not a melody; break the order.

**How you'll know you've got it.** Four two-bar answers to four different calls,
each matching the call's rhythm and ending settled, and one chorus built entirely
from a single three-note motif.
