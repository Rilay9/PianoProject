Hymn playing is the most efficient harmony training available, because every
chord is spelled out and there are four of them a bar.

**Four-part (SATB) texture.** A hymn is written as four voices — soprano, alto,
tenor, bass — with the top two on the treble staff and the lower two on the
bass. The right hand takes soprano and alto, the left takes tenor and bass. Read
it as four lines, not as two chords: each voice moves smoothly, mostly by step,
and seeing that makes the page far easier than it looks.

**The soprano is the tune.** It must be audible above the other three. This is
voicing (see the beautiful-pieces module) and in a hymn it is the whole job.

**The plagal cadence.** IV–I, the "amen". It is the sound of this repertoire and
it is worth playing a dozen times in different keys to fix it in the ear.

**Gospel passing chords.** Where a hymn holds one chord for two beats, gospel
fills the gap. The two simplest devices:

- **Walk-up in the bass.** Instead of holding the root, walk from one chord's
  root to the next by step or by chromatic step — C, D, D♯, E under a C-to-C/E
  change. The right hand holds; the bass moves.
- **Chromatic passing chord.** Slide the whole chord up or down a half step into
  the target chord and release it immediately. A half-step approach from below
  works nearly everywhere.

Both are decorations on a harmony that is already correct. Get the hymn right
first; add the passing chords afterwards.

**Repertoire.** *When the Saints* in C and F, *Greensleeves* with chords for
minor-key harmony, and the hymn tunes that come in through import.

**Common mistake.** Playing four parts at equal volume, which produces a wash.
Balance: soprano loudest, bass next, inner voices quiet.

**How you'll know you've got it.** A hymn played with the tune clearly on top,
plus one verse with a bass walk-up into each new chord.
