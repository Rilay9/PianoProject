**Not bundled.** In copyright; import your own MusicXML.

**What the piano part has to do.** A steady eighth-note keyboard figure over a
bass that holds, with the harmony changing under an almost static right hand.
This is the defining Linkin Park piano texture and it turns up across their
catalogue.

**The techniques.**

- **Eighth-note ostinato.** A two- or three-note figure repeated exactly, in
  strict time, for bars at a stretch. The difficulty is not the notes; it is
  playing them identically for a minute without accelerating. Metronome, always.
- **Pedal bass.** The lowest note stays put while chords change above it, which
  produces suspensions for free — a held C under an F chord gives you Fadd9 or
  F6/9 depending on the voicing.
- **Suspended chords.** With the third removed, the chord is neither major nor
  minor and can sit under a vocal without committing. sus2 and sus4 from
  chords-and-pop Stage 5.
- **Layering.** In the band the piano is one of several parts. Reduced to solo
  piano, decide whether you are playing the keyboard part or the whole
  arrangement; they are different pieces.

**Public-domain vehicles.** Bach *Prelude in C minor* BWV 999 (ostinato and
stamina); Chopin *Prélude* op. 28 no. 4 (harmony creeping under a repeated
right hand); Satie *Gnossienne no. 1* (open, unresolved voicings).

**What to listen for in the recording.** Whether the piano figure changes at all
between verse and chorus. Usually it does not — the growth comes from what is
added around it.

**Common mistake.** Adding expression to an ostinato. It is meant to be
machine-steady; the expression belongs to whatever is above it.

**How you'll know you've got it.** Two bars of ostinato repeated for two minutes
at a fixed tempo, unchanged, while the left hand changes harmony under it.
