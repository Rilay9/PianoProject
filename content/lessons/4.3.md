**An inversion** is the same chord with a different note at the bottom. C major
has three positions:

- **Root position** C–E–G
- **First inversion** E–G–C (take the bottom note up an octave)
- **Second inversion** G–C–E (do it again)

Same three letters, same chord, different shape and different sound. Written as
a **slash chord** when the bass matters: C/E means a C chord with E in the bass.

**Why they exist.** Root-position chords make the hand leap. Inversions let you
keep common tones and move one or two fingers instead of three: C (C–E–G) to
F/C (C–F–A) moves two fingers a step. That is the same idea as Stage 3's voice
leading, now available for every chord in every key. Once inversions are
automatic, chord charts stop being an obstacle course.

**Recognising them by shape.** Root position is the even one — three notes with
equal gaps. First inversion has its wide gap on top; second inversion has it on
the bottom. You can identify any inversion at a glance from where the fourth is,
without naming the notes.

**Arpeggio fingering.** An arpeggio is a chord played one note at a time across
two or more octaves. Right hand **1-2-3-5**, left hand **5-3-2-1**, with the
thumb passing under between groups exactly as in a scale — but further, so the
arm has to travel. Let the elbow lead the hand along the keyboard rather than
stretching the fingers to reach.

**What to do at the piano.** The inversion drill (the app shows "F/A", you play
it), then arpeggios hands separately, two octaves, in C, G, F, A minor, D minor
and E minor. Then a piece that uses broken chords throughout — the easy *Canon
in D*, or *Greensleeves*.

**Common mistake.** Learning inversions as three separate things to memorise per
chord. There are only three shapes, and they are the same three shapes in every
key. Learn the shapes.

**How you'll know you've got it.** Thirty random inversions at 95 %, each within
three seconds of seeing the symbol.
