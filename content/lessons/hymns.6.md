Everything below this rung handed you a hymn and left part of the job open: the
four voices to balance, the bass to walk. Here somebody else has done all of
it. These are piano pieces made out of hymn tunes, with every note of the
accompaniment written, and not one of them prints a chord symbol.

**Find the tune before you learn the notes.** In an arrangement the melody is
usually the top note of a right-hand chord, and it is easy to practise the
chords for a fortnight without ever having played the tune. Play the top line
alone first, through the whole piece, and only then put the rest under it. The
voicing study is that problem with nothing else in the way: four chords over a
single bass note, and one instruction printed on the score — the top note
sings, the rest accompany it.

**The left hand is the arrangement.** Look at what it does before you read it:
held chords, an off-beat chord after a low bass note, a bass that walks between
the changes. Once you can name the pattern, most of a page is that pattern
repeated and the reading is half as long.

**Where the tune stops, somebody wrote something.** A hymn holds the home chord
for two bars at the end of a line, and an arranger fills that gap with chords
the hymnal never had. The turnaround study is the smallest version — four
chords in two bars where the plain version has one — and it is the shape to
listen for at the end of every phrase here.

**Repertoire.** Four, easiest first. *Holy holy holy* is the short one at
sixteen bars, and it changes from four-four into six-four at its ninth bar and
stays there; from there it alternates a bar of repeated eighths with a bar that
holds. *10,000 Reasons* is in G, sixty-two bars, with the melody on top of
three-note chords and octaves low down. *Amazing Grace* in G is eighty-seven
bars against nineteen for the four-part setting two rungs below, which tells
you how much of it is the arranger's; it finishes on a run up to a single note
and not on its home chord. *Down by the Riverside* is the tune from the rung
below with the walk-up written out for you — sixty-six bars in cut time, marked
to be swung, with the left hand striding between a bass note and a chord.

**Tools for this rung.** *Accompaniment lab* opens the ballad bed: four chords
with a broken-chord left hand, no right hand at all, in whatever key you pick.
Play a hymn tune over it and you are arranging one, without a page to get in
the way. When a piece is ready, *Perform* on the score screen takes one pass,
start to finish, with no restarts and no loop, and keeps it as a performance
rather than as practice.

**Common mistake.** Treating the arrangement as the hymn. It is one person's
reading of it, and the parts that are hardest to play are often the parts they
added. If a bar defeats you for a week, the tune underneath it is four notes
and you are allowed to simplify what surrounds them.

**How you'll know you've got it.** You can play the melody of one of these on
its own from memory, and you can say in one sentence what its left hand is
doing — and when you play the whole thing, a listener hears the hymn rather
than the accompaniment.
