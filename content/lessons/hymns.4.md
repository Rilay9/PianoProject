A hymn is four singers written on two staves. Soprano and alto go to the right
hand, tenor and bass to the left, and in every piece on this rung each of the
four is printed as its own line rather than as a chord for the hand to grab.
Not one of them carries a chord symbol; there is nothing to read but the notes.

**Read four lines, not two chords.** The shape under your right hand is a
by-product of two voices that were written separately, and it will be an
awkward shape about once a line. Follow the soprano through a phrase on its
own, then the alto, and the awkward shape stops being a surprise: it is where
one voice held still while the other moved. This is the whole difference
between reading a hymn and grabbing at one.

**The two lines nobody practises are the ones your thumbs play.** Alto and
tenor move mostly by step and mostly by a half or a whole tone, because the
person harmonising the tune wanted them to. Where they jump, something has
happened. The triad study is the reason: run one chord up through its
inversions and watch which note has to move — that note is the inner voice, and
that is why it sits where it sits. The cadence study is four chords voiced so
the upper parts move as little as they can; it is written in the left hand
alone, three voices at a time, which is the easier half of the job.

**Every voice has to be joined.** Four parts at the piano fail as legato before
they fail as notes: a finger lifts early somewhere in the middle and a line
develops a hole nobody can point at. The legato study is one scale in the right
hand with one instruction printed on it — hold each key until the next one
sounds — and it is that instruction applied to one line at a time.

**Repertoire.** Five, easiest first. *Amazing Grace* in four parts is in G, in
three, and nineteen bars, and is the gentlest way in. *Rock of Ages* is in B
flat and in six-four, so the four lines move slowly enough to watch. *Abide
with Me* is in E flat; listen for the alto in its eleventh bar, which does
something the outer two voices do not. *Joyful, Joyful* is the tune you played
from a single line at Stage 2, here with three parts added under it. *O Sacred
Head* is Bach, forty bars, no sharps or flats, and by far the longest of the
five — the last thing to take on.

**Tools for this rung.** *Play it as a duet* opens *Amazing Grace* in four
parts with the right-hand staff yours, soprano and alto; the *Duet* row on the
score screen plays the hand you are not on, so tenor and bass arrive
underneath. Then use **L** on the control bar to swap, because the left-hand
pair is the half that never gets rehearsed.

**Common mistake.** Playing all four at the same weight, which produces a wash
with no tune in it. Soprano loudest, bass next, the middle two quiet enough
that you have to listen for them.

**How you'll know you've got it.** You can play one of these through with the
tune clearly on top, and you can play the left-hand staff alone, both of its
voices joined, without the page in front of you feeling like a wall of chords.
