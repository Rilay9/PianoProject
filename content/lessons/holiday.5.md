The carol rungs below this one handed you a tune and asked you to put something
under it. Here the arranging is already done: both hands are written out, note
for note, the way somebody meant them to sound. What is left is playing it, and
that turns out to be the harder job.

**Most of a written carol is one figure.** *Carol of the Bells* is the clearest
case there is — four notes, over and over, in thirty-three of its forty bars,
while the left hand moves one note at a time underneath. A figure that repeats
is not easier than a figure that changes. It is only shorter to learn, and it
will show every unevenness you have.

**So find the bar that is not the figure, and loop it.** Open *Loop* on the
score screen — double-tap the sheet to mark the bars — and play those two bars
until they are as automatic as the thirty-three around them. Playing the whole
piece from the top is how the easy bars get practised eleven times each and the
hard one gets practised once.

**Then let the speed be decided for you.** *Ladder*, on the same screen and
only once a loop is set, raises the tempo a notch after each clean pass and
drops it a notch after a pass with a mistake in it. It is the cure for the
thing everyone does with these pieces, which is to settle at the speed they can
already manage and stay there.

**The exercises are that figure with the music taken away.** The A minor
ostinato is eight bars of one broken chord that never changes — the score says
so on the page — in the key *Carol of the Bells* is in. The E major arpeggio is
the shape the left hand of *We Wish You a Merry Christmas* spells out, hands
together. Two-against-one in C is the plain version of the whole problem: one
hand keeping a steady rhythm while the other does not.

**Repertoire.** Four options — three for Christmas and one for New Year's Eve.

- *Carol of the Bells* is in A minor and in three, forty bars, and nearly all of
  it is that four-note figure. Start here.
- *We Wish You a Merry Christmas* is a full piano setting: E major, four sharps,
  in three, twenty-five bars, with the chord symbols printed above it as well.
  Its left hand is a six-note broken chord in most bars and a held chord in the
  rest.
- *Auld Lang Syne* is in F and in four, twenty bars, and both hands are in
  thirds and sixths nearly throughout. Nothing here repeats; the work is making
  a thick texture sound like one line.
- *Mary Did You Know* is the long one, sixty-two bars in B minor, and the
  slowest. It is where the Ladder earns its keep.

**Common mistake.** Learning the figure and calling the piece learned. The
figure is the part you will play well on the first afternoon; the piece is the
two bars you keep flinching at.

**How you'll know you've got it.** You can play one of these from the first bar
to the last without the tempo sagging at the hard bar — and without it having
been slow everywhere else to hide that.
