Jazz needs the chord vocabulary from chords-and-pop Stage 5 first. Given that,
three things get you playing.

**Swing eighths.** Same long-short division as the blues shuffle, usually a
little less extreme, and with the accent on the *off*-beat — the "and", not the
number. That accent is what separates swing from a shuffle. Play a scale in
eighths and lean on every "and".

**Shell voicings.** A seventh chord has four notes; the two that define it are
the **third and the seventh**. The root is the bass player's job and the fifth
says almost nothing. So the left hand plays **root and seventh**, or **root and
third**, and that is a shell — two notes, unambiguous harmony, and room for the
right hand.

Why it works: the third tells you major or minor, the seventh tells you whether
it is a dominant. Between them they identify the chord; everything else is
decoration.

**ii–V–I.** The backbone progression. In C: **Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7**. Voice it as
shells and watch what happens — the seventh of Dm7 (C) falls a half step to the
third of G7 (B), and the seventh of G7 (F) falls a half step to the third of
Cmaj7 (E). Those two falling half steps are **guide-tone resolution**, and they
are why the progression sounds inevitable. Learn ii–V–I in C, F, B flat and G,
with the shells barely moving.

**Comping.** Accompanying: chords played in rhythm, in the gaps, not on every
beat. Start with a chord on beat 1 and the "and" of 2, and leave the rest alone.

**Repertoire for this rung.** *Ja-Da* (1918), *After You've Gone* (1918),
*Avalon* (1920), *Bill Bailey* (1902), *Some of These Days* (1910) — all US
public domain, all short forms.

**Common mistake.** Playing four-note rootless voicings before the shells are
automatic. The shells are what you fall back on when the tune moves fast.

**How you'll know you've got it.** ii–V–I in four keys with shell voicings, and
one standard comped through twice while you sing the melody.
