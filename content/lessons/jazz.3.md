Jazz starts a rung before the chords do. Nothing here asks you to build a chord
or read a symbol. This rung is the *feel*, on four tunes and five short studies.

**Two eighths, unequal.** Written evenly, played long-short: the first takes
about two thirds of the beat and the second about one third. The three studies
called *Straight, then swung* are four bars played dead even, a bar of silence,
and the same four bars again with the swing marked — one each in C, F and G.
Play the two halves back to back and listen to the join. The short note is
**late, not early**; it leans towards the beat that follows it.

**Rhythm before notes.** The shuffle study is four bars of rhythm on a single
line with nothing else in it. Clap it, then tap it on one key. Neither hand has
a second job on this rung.

**Playing a phrase back.** *Answer the phrase* plays you two bars from a
five-note scale and wants the same two bars back, note for note. That is
imitation and not invention — making up an answer of your own comes later, on
the improvisation ladder and again when you are trading phrases with another
player. What this trains is what both of those rest on: holding two bars in
your head long enough to play them.

**The letters above the stave.** All four tunes print chord symbols, the letter
names of the harmony written over the melody for whoever is accompanying.
**Leave them alone here.** They are what the Stage 4 jazz rung comps from, and
reading a tune while ignoring what is printed over it is its own small skill.

**Repertoire for this rung.** Four options, every one a melody on a single
stave from before 1930, chosen for one reason: they have eighth notes in them
to swing. *Swing Low, Sweet Chariot* is the short one — sixteen bars in G over
three chords, and eleven of those sixteen bars have a run of eighths.
*Alexander's Ragtime Band* is the odd one: twenty-one of its thirty-three bars
write the long-short out as a dotted eighth and a sixteenth, which is sharper
than swing but is the same idea printed on the page. *Bye Bye Blackbird* is the
one with the most chord changes over it, fifty-seven in sixty-three bars.
*Ole Miss* is the long one, sixty-four bars, and its runs climb chromatically
rather than through the key. More at this level under Jazz and under Blues in
the Library.

**Tools for this rung.** No button here opens anything, because what this rung
wants are two of the score screen's own controls rather than a mode of its own.
*Rhythm only*, in Keep tempo, judges your timing and not your notes — one tap
per written note or chord, on any key at all — which is precisely what swing is.
*Speed* slows the tune down until the long and the short are plainly different
lengths. Set the second, then the first.

**Common mistake.** Swinging the quarter notes as well. Only the eighths move.
A tune bounced from end to end sounds like a limp rather than a lilt, and the
giveaway is that you can no longer hear where the beat is.

**How you'll know you've got it.** One of these tunes played through twice —
once dead even, once swung — and you can hear which was which without counting
either of them.
