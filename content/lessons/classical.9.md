There is no rung above this one, and nothing here is meant to be passed. These
are pieces to live with — ten minutes of music, several months of work, and a
result that keeps changing for years afterwards.

**Pick one. Not three.** The single most common way to waste a year is to start
four large pieces and finish none. Choose the one you cannot stop thinking
about, and let the others wait; they will still be here.

**Learn it from the score, not from a recording.** By all means listen — but
after you have made your own decisions about tempo, dynamics and pedalling,
because a recording heard first becomes the version you copy without noticing.
Mark your own phrasing on the score before you play a note of it.

**Sections, backwards, out of order.** The Stage 8 habit matters more here, not
less. A ballade is fifteen sections wearing a trench coat. Number them, learn
the last first, and practise them in a different order every day.

**Practise the transitions separately.** In a long Romantic piece the joins are
where performances fall apart, because they are the bars nobody repeats. Make a
list of the four bars either side of every section change and treat that list as
its own piece.

**Slow practice never stops being the answer.** At this level slow practice is
not for learning the notes — you know them — but for checking that the hand is
still doing what you think it is doing. One slow, quiet run-through a day,
listening for anything you have started to fake.

**What to play.** Seventeen options, and they are all long. Four ballades, three
scherzos, the Barcarolle, the F minor Fantaisie, the *Heroic* polonaise, the
Polonaise-fantaisie, the "Revolutionary" étude, three sonata movements — the
first and the funeral march from the B-flat minor, the finale of the B minor —
and Liszt's *La campanella* if you want a piece that is purely about the hands.

**Common mistake.** Playing it through from the top, every day, at whatever
tempo it happens to come out. That is performing, not practising, and it is how
a piece stops improving.

**How you'll know you've got it.** You can play it from four different starting
points, at a tempo you chose rather than one you can just about manage, and it
sounds like a decision rather than an accident.
