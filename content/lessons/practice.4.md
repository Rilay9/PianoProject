Pianists do get playing-related pain and injuries, and studies of them point to
several things together — long sessions without rest, a lot of repetition, the
set-up of bench and hands, hand size — rather than one cause, with no agreement
yet on how much each matters. What you can do is notice early signs, such as
tiredness, tension or discomfort, and take them seriously before they become
pain.

**Warming up.** Start with something easy and slow before anything hard. A few
minutes is usually enough, and the easy start is also a chance to decide what
this session is for.

**Tension.** Three places it often shows: shoulders creeping up towards your
ears, a forearm that feels hard when you touch it with the other hand, and
holding your breath. Each is easy to let go of once you notice it.

Telling yourself to relax rarely helps on its own. What often does: play more
slowly, let the weight of the arm do some of the work instead of pressing with
the fingers, and let go of each key once it has sounded.

**Pain — stop.** Not "finish this passage", and not at the end of the bar.
Stop. Any pain in the wrist, forearm, thumb or shoulder means the session is
over; rest, and make the next one shorter or gentler.

Pain that lasts more than a couple of days, or any numbness or tingling, is a
reason to see a doctor or a physiotherapist, not a practice problem. Nothing
about the piano is worth an injury that takes months.

**Common mistake.** Long sessions at weekends after a week of none. Long
practice without rest is one of the risk factors those studies report, so break a
long session into shorter stretches with rests between them.

**How you'll know you've got it.** You notice tension before it becomes
discomfort, and you have stopped at least once because you were tired.
