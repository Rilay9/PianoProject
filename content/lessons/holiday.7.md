The carols stop here. What is left is the other half of December: the music
people expect at a winter concert and nobody sings along to. Two of these are
orchestral numbers put on to a piano; the third is a jazz waltz written for a
Christmas television special.

**An orchestral reduction hides its difficulty in the left hand.** The right
hand gets the tune everybody knows, and it is usually the easy part. The left
hand is carrying what a string section and a bass were doing between them — a
low note, then a chord, then the same chord again, in time, every bar, for
eighty bars. Use *Hands* on the score screen, which chooses which hand the app
waits for, and practise the left alone until it is boring.

**The three exercises are that left hand taken apart.** The rotation study is
the left hand on its own in sixteenths, rocking between a low note and the notes
above it. The four-to-a-note study is one note struck again and again without
the arm getting heavy. The stride study holds a chord in the right hand and
gives the left the jump itself: bass note on the beat, chord after it, over
three chords in C.

**Then play it as a performance.** *Perform* is one pass, start to finish, with
no restarts and no loop, kept as a performance rather than as practice. A
recital piece that has only ever been played in pieces is not yet a recital
piece.

**Repertoire.** Three options, each with its left hand written out in full.

- *Waltz of the Flowers* is in D, eighty bars in three, and its first four bars
  are left hand alone — a low D, then the chord twice. That is the whole
  accompaniment, and it does not let up.
- *Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy* is in E minor and in two, fifty-three bars.
  The left hand begins as a low octave struck against the rests and becomes a
  bass-and-chord alternation once the tune arrives above it.
- *Skating* is the odd one out and by far the longest: C major, a hundred and
  thirty-seven bars in three, marked to be swung, with chord symbols printed
  over the whole of it. Its left hand is stride in three.

**Common mistake.** Bringing the right hand up to speed and leaving the left
where it was. In all three of these the left hand decides the tempo you can
actually hold, and it will not be argued with.

**How you'll know you've got it.** You can play the left hand of one of these
alone, in time, at the speed you want the piece — and then play the piece at
that speed without it getting heavier as it goes.
