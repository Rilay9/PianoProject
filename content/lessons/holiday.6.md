There is a short list of carols that quiet a room, and every piece here is a
concert setting of one: the tune lives inside the chords rather than sitting on
top of them, and the whole thing is meant to be heard through once.

**Playing something through once is a different skill from practising it.**
*Perform* on the score screen is one pass, start to finish, with no restarts and
no loop, and it is kept as a performance rather than as practice. Do it before
you feel ready. What a performance teaches is what happens after a mistake, and
you cannot learn that by stopping.

**Make the top note louder than the rest of the chord.** In settings like these
the melody is one voice of a right-hand chord, and if all the notes arrive at
the same weight the tune disappears into its own harmony. The voicing exercise
in D is four chords and one instruction: the top note sings, the rest accompany
it.

**The pedal joins the harmony; it must not blur it.** The held-melody exercise
holds one note in the right hand over changing chords in the left, and asks you
to change the pedal underneath without that held note breaking. It is the whole
of the pedalling these pieces need, and you check it by listening for the held
note rather than for the chord.

**Then stop looking.** *Play it blind* hides the notation and keeps following
you. A room that is listening will not hold still while you find your place on
the page.

**Repertoire.** Four options, in order of how much of the tune is hidden.

- *Silent Night* is the short one: thirty bars in A flat, four flats, in three,
  and its right hand is chords from the first bar to the last with the melody as
  the top note of each. Learn this one by heart first.
- *Carol of the Bells* is the figure from the rung below, now in D minor and
  made into a real piece — sixty-five bars, chords in both hands, and the bottom
  of the keyboard used.
- *O Holy Night* is ninety-six bars in six-eight, starting in E major with four
  sharps and going to five before it comes back. Its left hand is a six-note
  broken chord, bar after bar, for most of the piece.
- *Joy to the World* is the loud one: two sharps, in two, seventy-three bars,
  with octaves in both hands and runs between them.

**Common mistake.** Practising the ending and starting the piece at the volume
habit leaves you at. If the last page is where it arrives, the first page has to
leave somewhere to arrive from.

**How you'll know you've got it.** You can play one of these through once, with
the notation hidden, and the tune is what a listener hears rather than the
accompaniment under it.
