Four chords played plainly are four chords. The same four with the right colour
are an arrangement.

**Sus chords have no third**, which is exactly why they work: with no third they
are neither major nor minor, so they sound like a chord waiting to become one.
`sus4` resolves down to the third; `sus2` often does not resolve at all and just
sits there sounding open. Both are one finger away from the triad you already
play.

**add9** is a major triad with the ninth added and the seventh left out. It is
the sound of most pop piano, and it is not a ninth chord — a ninth chord has the
seventh in it and sounds like jazz. Play C, Cadd9 and C9 in a row and the
difference is obvious and worth knowing.

**Where the colour goes.** Spread the notes. The same four pitches under one hand
are a block; the same four with the ninth up an octave are a texture. The open
voicings on this rung are written the second way on purpose.

**What to practise.** The four-chord loop with every chord played `add9`, then
with the first chord `sus4` resolving. Then the ninth chords from the jazz drill,
so you can hear what you are *not* doing.

**Repertoire for this rung.** A ballad you already play, re-voiced. Same chords,
different arrangement.

**Common mistake.** Adding colour to every chord. A sus that resolves is a
moment; a whole song of them is a drone.

**How you'll know you've got it.** You can take a plain chord chart and make it
sound like a record, without changing a single chord symbol.
