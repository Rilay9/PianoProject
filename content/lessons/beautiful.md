This is not a ladder; it is a shelf. The pieces here are the ones people learn
the piano *for*, and they are collected in one place so you can always see what
you are working towards and take one down when you are ready.

**What is on the shelf and bundled.** Satie *Gymnopédie no. 1* and *Gnossienne
no. 1*; Pachelbel *Canon in D* (easy and full); Bach *Air on the G String* and
*Prelude in C*; Chopin *Préludes* op. 28 nos. 4, 7 and 20, the *Nocturne* op. 9
no. 2 and the *Waltz in A minor*; Beethoven *Moonlight* first movement and *Für
Elise*; Debussy *Clair de Lune* and *Arabesque no. 1*; Brahms–Halvorsen
*Passacaglia*; Schubert–Liszt *Ständchen*; Liszt *Liebestraum no. 3*;
Tchaikovsky's *Swan Lake* and *Nutcracker* themes.

**What is not, and never will be.** Einaudi, Yiruma, Tiersen, Zimmer, Sakamoto,
Hisaishi — all in copyright. Buy the MusicXML or transcribe it, and import it;
it will sit on the same shelf.

**The three skills these pieces need**, none of which are speed:

1. **Tone.** A singing melody comes from arm weight transferred slowly into the
   key, not from striking it. Practise one note, listening, twenty times.
2. **Voicing.** Playing one note of a chord louder than the others, usually the
   top. Practise the chord with the melody note *only* sounding, then add the
   others quietly underneath.
3. **Pedalling.** Stage 3.5's legato pedalling, applied constantly and changed
   with the harmony. In this repertoire a late pedal change is the difference
   between beautiful and muddy.

**Choose one, not five.** The characteristic failure with this shelf is starting
six pieces that are all slightly too hard and finishing none. Take the easiest
thing here that you actually love, and finish it.

**Common mistake.** Learning the famous eight bars and stopping. The middle
section is where the piece is.

**How you'll know you've got it.** One piece from this shelf played all the way
through, from memory or not, that you would play for somebody.
