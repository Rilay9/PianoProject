A block chord is three notes at once. Break it up and the same three notes
become an accompaniment — a texture with movement underneath a tune. Three
patterns cover most of the repertoire from here to Stage 6.

**Broken chord (root–fifth–third–fifth).** Take C–E–G and play C, G, E, G as
even notes. It fills the bar without ever getting in the melody's way, and it
is the pattern behind a great deal of hymn and folk accompaniment.

**Alberti bass** is the eighteenth-century version: **lowest, highest, middle,
highest** — for C–E–G that is C, G, E, G in eighth notes, repeated. It is named
after Domenico Alberti and it is everywhere in Mozart and Clementi. Played
badly it sounds like a sewing machine; the fix is to lean very slightly on the
lowest note of each group and keep the other three light.

**Waltz bass ("oom-pah-pah")** is for 3/4: the root of the chord on beat one,
the rest of the chord on beats two and three. The bass note is the "oom" and
carries the weight; the two chord beats are the "pah-pah" and must be quieter,
or the accompaniment sits on top of the tune instead of under it.

**Changing chord under a pattern.** The pattern does not restart — it continues
and the notes inside it change. Practise the change on its own: two bars of C
pattern into two bars of F pattern, over and over, until the seam disappears.

**What to do at the piano.** Left-hand pattern drills in C, G, F and A minor
from 60 to 90 bpm, then the same drills with a right-hand scale over the top —
the point is independence, not the pattern. Then *Greensleeves (waltz bass)*.

**Common mistake.** Playing the accompaniment as loud as the melody, because
both hands are doing something interesting. The left hand should be roughly
half the volume of the right. Record yourself; it is worse than you think.

**How you'll know you've got it.** Two patterns in two keys at 80 bpm, and one
piece where you can hear the tune clearly above the left hand.
