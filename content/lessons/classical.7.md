Stage 6 asked you to hear one line above an accompaniment. This rung asks for
two things at once that are harder: **two lines of equal importance**, and **a
melody that is decorated rather than stated.**

**Counterpoint means both hands are the tune.** In Bach there is no
accompaniment. Each hand plays a line that would make sense on its own, and the
piece is what happens between them. Practise them as separate pieces first —
right hand alone until it is musical, not merely correct, then left hand the
same way. Only then together, slowly, listening for the moment one voice takes
over from the other. If you cannot sing the left hand from memory, you have not
learned it.

**Articulation is the expression.** Bach wrote almost no dynamics and the
harpsichord had none to speak of, so shape comes from *how long each note lasts*
— detached, joined, or somewhere between. Choose an articulation for each voice
and keep it consistent every time that figure returns. Inconsistency is what
makes Bach sound like an exercise.

**Sonata form is a map, not a rule.** K. 545's first movement states two themes,
takes them somewhere else, and brings them back. Knowing where the boundaries
are tells you what to practise: the joins. The exposition is usually the part
everyone can play and the development is the part nobody can, because it is the
only part that is not repeated.

**The ornamented melody.** A Chopin nocturne writes the tune once plainly and
then again with a spray of small notes over it. Those runs are *not* in strict
time and they are not accelerandos either — they are a decoration of one long
note, and they have to arrive at the next main beat with the left hand. Practise
by playing the plain version first: strip the ornament out, learn where the
melody notes fall, then put the decoration back on top of a line you can already
hear.

**Alberti bass, and keeping it quiet.** A broken-chord left hand should be
inaudible as a pattern and audible as a harmony. Play it with a flat, still hand
and almost no finger action; if you hear four separate notes, it is too loud.

**What to play.** Twenty-two options. The Chopin group is the widest: four
preludes at this level, five nocturnes — including the E minor Op. 72, the one
he wrote as a teenager and the gentlest way into the style — three waltzes and
three mazurkas. Beside them: Mozart's K. 545 first movement for sonata form,
the C minor prelude from the Forty-Eight for finger evenness, the first movement
of the *Moonlight* and the *Pathétique* slow movement for sustained voicing, and
Satie's first Gnossienne, which has no bar lines at all and is a good cure for
counting instead of listening.

**Common mistake.** Taking the nocturnes too slowly. They are marked *Andante*
and *Larghetto*, not *Adagio*; below a certain tempo the long melody notes stop
connecting to each other and the piece stops being a line.

**How you'll know you've got it.** A Bach piece where a listener can follow
either hand by choice — and a nocturne where the ornament sounds like one
gesture rather than a scale.
