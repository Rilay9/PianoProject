Stage 6 asked you to hear one line above an accompaniment. This rung asks two harder
things at once: **two lines of equal importance**, and **a
melody that is decorated rather than stated.**

**Counterpoint means both hands are the tune.** In Bach there is no
accompaniment. Each hand plays a line that would make sense on its own, and the
piece is what happens between them. Practise them as separate pieces — each hand
alone until it is musical, not merely correct — and only then together, slowly,
listening for the moment one voice takes over. If you cannot sing the left hand from memory, you have not
learned it.

**Articulation is the expression.** Bach wrote almost no dynamics and the
harpsichord had none, so shape comes from *how long each note lasts* —
detached, joined, or between. Choose an articulation for each voice and keep it
consistent every time that figure returns; inconsistency is what makes Bach
sound like an exercise.

**Sonata form is a map, not a rule.** K. 545's first movement states two themes,
takes them somewhere else, and brings them back. Knowing where the boundaries
are tells you what to practise: the joins. The exposition is the part everyone can
play; the development is the part nobody can.

**The ornamented melody.** A Chopin nocturne writes the tune once plainly and
then again with a spray of small notes over it. Those runs are *not* in strict
time and not accelerandos either — they decorate one long note and have to
arrive at the next main beat with the left hand. Practise the plain version
first: strip the ornament out, learn where the melody notes fall, then put the
decoration back on a line you can already hear.

**Alberti bass, and keeping it quiet.** A broken-chord left hand should be
inaudible as a pattern and audible as a harmony: a flat, still hand and almost
no finger action. If you hear four separate notes, it is too loud.

**What to play.** Six options. Mozart's K. 545 first movement for sonata form;
the *Moonlight* first movement and the *Pathétique* slow movement for sustained
voicing; Chopin's Mazurka Op. 7 No. 1 for a singing line over a left hand
that leans on beat two or three — the mazurka's accent; and Bach's inventions
No. 1 in C and No. 4 in D minor, the two-voice work this rung is named for,
where one hand answers the other and neither may be louder than the line
demands. The other inventions, and Chopin's waltzes and nocturnes, are in the
Library.

**Common mistake.** Taking nocturnes too slowly. Op. 9 No. 2 is marked *Andante*
and No. 1 *Larghetto*, not *Adagio*; below a certain tempo the long melody notes stop
connecting and the piece stops being a line.

**Tools for this rung.** *Play it as a duet* opens Invention No. 1 with the
right hand yours and the app playing the other line at a level it never varies,
so you hear whether yours answers it or covers it. Swap with the *Hands* row:
the way round you find harder is the voice you have not learned. *Play it
blind* takes the first of the six instead, K. 545, and hides the notation while
still following and marking the run — a test of whether you know both
voices or only the one your eyes were on.

**How you'll know you've got it.** A Bach piece where a listener can follow
either hand by choice — and a nocturne from the Library where the ornament sounds like one
gesture rather than a scale.
