The staff holds nine pitches. The piano holds eighty-eight. **Ledger lines** are
the short lines drawn above or below the staff to carry the extra ones, and
counting them one at a time is slow, so you learn landmarks instead and read
from those.

**Five landmarks worth knowing cold:**

- **Middle C (C4)** — one ledger line below the treble staff, one above the bass.
- **Treble G (G4)** — second line up, the line the treble clef curls around.
- **Bass F (F3)** — fourth line up in the bass, between the clef's two dots.
- **High C (C6)** — two ledger lines above the treble staff.
- **Low C (C2)** — two ledger lines below the bass staff.

Anything else is one or two steps from a landmark, and one or two steps is a
distance your eye can judge without counting.

**8va** written above a passage means play it an octave higher than printed;
below a passage, or written *8vb*, means an octave lower. It exists to avoid
five ledger lines, and it applies until the dotted line ends.

**Both hands away from middle C.** Up to now the hands have lived either side of
middle C. Real pieces put them anywhere, and the two staves stop being "top
half" and "bottom half" of one small region. The fix is the same as it was
before: read the interval from the last note, and trust the landmark when you
lose the thread.

**What to do at the piano.** Note-flash extended from C2 to C6, sight-reading
level 2 (two hands, wider range, quarters and eighths), then the Petzold
*Minuet in G* — the first real piece in the plan, and one whose right hand
ranges well above the staff.

**Common mistake.** Counting ledger lines upward from the top line every time.
Learn High C and count from there — it is two steps, not five.

**How you'll know you've got it.** Five generated sight-reads at 85 %, and one
piece learned to tempo.
