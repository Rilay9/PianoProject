**What the piano part has to do.** This is the one where the piano is the lead
instrument rather than support, which changes the problem: the part is exposed,
so evenness and voicing are audible, and there is nowhere to hide a rushed bar.

**The techniques.**

- **Two-hand independence.** A repeating left-hand figure under a right hand
  that phrases freely. The left hand must be automatic — practise it alone until
  it runs while you talk.
- **Minor harmony with modal colour.** Minor-key rock frequently uses the
  flattened seventh rather than a raised leading tone, which is Aeolian or
  Dorian rather than harmonic minor. It is why the harmony can circle without
  ever resolving. Theory-and-ear Stage 5 covers this.
- **Rubato within a pulse.** A piano-led verse can breathe; the trick is that
  time borrowed must be paid back, so the bar still lasts a bar.

**Public-domain vehicles.** Bach *Prelude in C minor* BWV 999 for an unbroken
figure under a slow harmonic line; Satie *Gnossienne no. 1* for free phrasing
over a steady left hand; *Greensleeves in 6/8* for modal minor colour.

**What to listen for in the recording.** Whether the piano's left hand is doing
the bass guitar's job or something of its own, and where the phrase endings sit
relative to the drums.

**Common mistake.** Practising the expressive right hand before the left hand is
mechanical. Freedom in one hand requires reliability in the other.

**How you'll know you've got it.** The BWV 999 figure running evenly for a page
while you shape the upper line, without the left hand following the right.
