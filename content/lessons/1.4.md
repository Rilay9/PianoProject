Piano music is written on two staves joined by a **brace** — the **grand
staff**. Treble on top for the right hand, bass underneath for the left, and
middle C living in the gap between them, one ledger line below the top staff or
one above the bottom.

The key skill is reading **vertically**: everything printed in the same column
happens at the same moment. Your eye has to take in a slice of time, not a line
of notes. In this unit the hands never play together — the left hand answers the
right, call and response — so you can practise the vertical scan without also
having to coordinate two hands.

**3/4 time** has three beats in a bar instead of four; count "1 2 3". A **dotted
half note** lasts three beats: a dot after any note adds half of that note's
value again, so 2 + 1 = 3. That rule holds for every dotted note you will ever
meet.

**An anacrusis** (or upbeat) is when a tune starts before beat one — the first
bar is short and the missing beats are at the end of the piece. *When the
Saints* starts on beat two with a three-beat upbeat, which is why the long note
lands on beat one of the next bar and the answering bars begin with a rest.
Count the missing beats in your head before you play: "1 — 2 3 4" and in on 2.

**What to do at the piano.** Alternating-hand patterns first, then *Ode to Joy
(hands alternating)* — right hand asks, left hand answers — and *When the
Saints*. Keep the resting hand on its keys; do not lift it into the air between
turns.

**Common mistake.** Reading the two staves as two separate lines, finishing the
top one and then hunting for where the bottom one had got to. Read down the
column, always.

**How you'll know you've got it.** One alternating-hands tune at 70 bpm at 90 %,
with the handover landing exactly on the beat and no gap where you switch.
