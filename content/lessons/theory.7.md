Every key has seven chords. Music uses more than seven, and this is how the
extra ones are named.

**A secondary dominant is a dominant of something that is not the tonic.** In C,
`V/V` is D major — the dominant of G — and the F sharp in it is the note that
tells you the music has briefly left home. Written as a numeral it is two
characters; described in words it takes a sentence, which is the argument for
numerals in miniature.

**Tonicisation is not modulation.** A secondary dominant points at a chord for a
moment and then the music carries on in the original key. A modulation stays.
The difference is length, and your ear knows it long before your analysis does.

**Chord-scales.** Every chord suggests a scale: a dominant seventh takes
mixolydian, a minor seventh dorian, a half-diminished chord locrian. Those three
are not opinions — they are what the chord tones already spell. Where there is a
choice (a major seventh takes ionian or lydian depending on what it is doing),
the app names one and says so, and you should trust your ear over it.

**What to practise.** The secondary-dominant ear drill until `V/V` is a sound and
not a calculation, and one chord-scale a day played through two octaves.

**Repertoire for this rung.** None required; the ear drills are the rung.

**Common mistake.** Analysing every chromatic chord as a secondary dominant. Some
of them are borrowed from the minor and some are just chromatic.

**How you'll know you've got it.** You hear a chord that is not in the key and
know which chord it is pointing at.
