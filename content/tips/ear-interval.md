## What it's for

Naming the distance between two notes by ear. Everything else in ear training
is built on it: a chord is a stack of intervals and a melody is a chain of them.
The first drill asks only step or skip — a 2nd or a 3rd — and the later one
asks for every interval inside the octave.

## How to practise it

Anchor each interval to a tune you already know — a fourth is the start of *Here
Comes the Bride*, a fifth is *Twinkle*, a minor third is the cuckoo, a major
second is the start of *Happy Birthday*. Sing the anchor before you answer.
Singing, not humming inwardly: the voice is a far more reliable guide than the
ear on its own.

On the two-choice drill, sing the two notes and hear whether there is a note
between them: a step has none, a skip has one.

On the full set, work on the ones you get wrong. Most people confuse the fourth
with the fifth and the major sixth with the minor seventh, and drilling the ones
you already know feels good and teaches nothing.

## Common mistake

Answering from the *first* note's pitch rather than from the gap. If the drill
transposes and your accuracy collapses, that is what has been happening.

## How you'll know you've got it

You name the common intervals without reaching for the anchor tune, and the
awkward pairs no longer swap.
